# Portfolio content that sounds like a person wrote it

The reader is a recruiter or design lead with 60 seconds and 40 tabs open. They skim headings, look at the work, and read only the sentences that earn it. Write for that person: specific, short, first person, and honest.

## The core rules

1. **Say what you did, in the first person.** Write "I designed the onboarding," not "The onboarding was designed" or "Our solution enabled." Use "we" only for genuine team work, and then say who did what.
2. **Specific beats impressive.** Put a number, a name, a place, a date or a real quote in place of an adjective. Not "significantly improved conversion" but "orders went from 12 to 30 a month."
3. **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has two commas and an "and," split it.
4. **Use plain words.** Write it the way you'd explain it to a friend over chai. If you wouldn't say it out loud, don't write it.
5. **Cut anything the reader can't check or doesn't need.** Every line should be either a fact, a decision, or a result.
6. **Be honest about the mess.** What went wrong, what you'd change, what got cut, and what the client did after you left. Real projects have rough edges, and showing them builds more trust than polish.
7. **The picture does the describing.** Don't write a paragraph describing a screen that's shown right next to it. Say why it looks that way.

## Banned words and phrases (these mark copy as AI-written)

Never use these:
- leverage, utilize, seamless, seamlessly, robust, elevate, empower, delve, unlock, harness, streamline, cutting-edge, innovative, game-changer, revolutionize, transformative, holistic, synergy, best-in-class, world-class
- "in today's fast-paced world," "in the ever-evolving landscape," "at the intersection of," "a testament to," "navigate the complexities," "a deep dive," "meticulously crafted," "pixel-perfect," "user-centric approach," "end-to-end journey," "delightful experience," "intuitive and engaging"
- "passionate about," "I believe design can change the world," "storyteller," "problem solver" (as a self-label)
- "Not only X, but also Y," "It's not just X, it's Y," "More than just a…"
- Fake-dramatic openers like "Imagine a world where…" and "What if I told you…"
- Wrap-up closers like "In conclusion," "Ultimately," "At the end of the day," "This project taught me the importance of…"

## Patterns to avoid (even when the words are fine)

- **Groups of three by default.** "Fast, simple, and beautiful." Real writing uses two things, or four, or one. Use a group of three only when there really are three.
- **The "X, not Y" reflex.** "Story first, screens second." "Built for owners, not admins." Once on a page is a voice; three times is a template.
- **The same sentence formula on every page.** For example, every case study opening with "The hardest part was…" or every decision having identical "Why:" and "Trade-off:" boxes, word for word. Vary how you open sentences and sections.
- **Em-dash chains.** No more than one dash per paragraph. Use a full stop instead.
- **Colon headlines.** "Onboarding: Reimagined." "Trust: The Foundation." Avoid them.
- **Title Case For Every Heading.** Use sentence case.
- **Rhetorical questions as section openers.** "So how do you build trust?" Just say how.
- **Hedging stacks.** "This could potentially help to somewhat improve…" Commit to it, or cut it.
- **Everything is a "journey," "story" or "experience."** Name the actual thing: the sign-up flow, the product page, the checkout.
- **Balanced, symmetrical paragraphs of the same length.** Real writing is uneven. A one-line paragraph is fine.
- **Emoji and decorative symbols in body copy.**

## Headings

- A heading should be readable on its own and say what you did or what changed. Example: "Turned building a website into a WhatsApp chat."
- Use a verb in the past tense for your work: designed, rebuilt, cut, replaced, shipped.
- Keep it to 10 words or fewer on cards and 2 lines or fewer on page headings.
- If there's a strong result, put it in the heading or right under it. Example: "A brand's own store that earned 7.85x ROAS."
- Put the problem in the first paragraph, not the heading.
- Test it: if the heading could sit on any other designer's portfolio unchanged, rewrite it.

| Generic (rewrite) | Specific (good) |
|---|---|
| Reimagining the e-commerce experience | Gave watch buyers a reason to skip Amazon |
| Designing for trust and community | Designed the gate that makes a supercar network worth joining |
| A seamless onboarding journey | Cut sign-up to one WhatsApp message |
| Elevating a luxury brand | Made a stone brand architects will specify |

## Case study structure

Keep it short. A recruiter should get the whole story from the headings and the first line of each section.

1. **Heading.** What you did (see above).
2. **One-line summary.** What the product is, who it's for, and what you owned. No more than 2 sentences.
3. **Facts table.** Role, team (with real roles), timeline (real months), platform, and outcome. Never leave a stand-in like "[dates]"; delete the row instead.
4. **The problem.** 2 to 4 sentences. Whose problem, what it cost them, and how you know. Use a number or a real quote if you have one.
5. **What you found.** 2 or 3 findings from research, each one sentence, each tied to a decision later on.
6. **Decisions.** 3 to 6 numbered decisions. For each: what you chose, what you didn't choose, and why. Write it as a short paragraph rather than identical labelled boxes, or use labels only where they actually help.
7. **Outcome.** Numbers first, then quotes, then what happened after. Say who else contributed to a result ("ROAS shared with the marketing team who ran the ads"). If there are no numbers, say so plainly and give what you do have, such as "Live and selling. We didn't set targets, so I can't show conversion."
8. **What I'd do differently.** 1 to 3 real points. Not "I learned the value of collaboration."

Aim for about 600–1,200 words a case study. If it's longer, cut the process narration first.

## Results and claims

- Every number must be real and checkable. Give the source and time frame: "500+ builds in the two months after the August 2026 launch."
- Credit the team where results were shared. Recruiters ask about this in interviews.
- Don't claim impact you can't trace to your work.
- If a project was paused, rolled back or changed direction, say so in one calm sentence. Hiding it reads worse when someone finds out.
- Quotes must be real and attributed (name, role). Keep them short.

## Hero, about and microcopy

- **Hero:** name plus one sentence on what you do and what's different about you. Read it out loud; if it sounds like a LinkedIn headline, rewrite it.
- **About:** short, first person, with one or two specific human details (the cat app, the open mics) rather than a list of traits.
- **Buttons and links:** say what happens, like "Read the case study," "Download resume (PDF)" or "Email me." Not "Explore," "Discover" or "Learn more."
- **Humour:** dry and specific, never forced. One line per section at most.

## Editing pass (run on every page before shipping)

1. Search for every banned word and phrase above. Remove them.
2. Search for `[`, `TBC`, `TODO`, `YOUR_`, `lorem`. None of these may ship.
3. Count groups of three and "X, not Y" lines per page. Keep at most one of each.
4. Read every heading on its own, top to bottom. Does it tell the story of the project? Could it belong to anyone else?
5. Read it aloud. Rewrite any sentence you stumble on or wouldn't say.
6. Cut 20%. The first draft is always too long.
7. Check the facts match across pages: role names, dates, team size, outcome wording.
8. Make sure no paragraph describes something the image next to it already shows.

## Quick before and after

**Before:** "In this project, I leveraged a user-centric approach to reimagine the onboarding journey, creating a seamless and delightful experience that empowered small business owners to effortlessly build their online presence."

**After:** "Owners start on WhatsApp. No sign-up, no form. They answer questions about their business, never about design, and the site builds while they watch. 290 sites went live in the first two months."
