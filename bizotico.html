Work

Bizotico, now The Timekeepers

Designing a watch distributor's own store to compete with the marketplaces selling its brands.

E-commerce Designer · Bizotico, Bengaluru · 6 months · 2023 to 2024

In short

The same watches were a tap away on Amazon, so the distributor's own store needed a reason to exist.

Role. E-commerce Designer at Bizotico, Bengaluru, six months across 2023 and 2024. I led one graphic designer and worked alongside the marketing team.
The decisions that matter. Four ways into one catalogue, so a ₹4,000 watch and a ₹60,000 watch can share it. A position built on what only a distributor has: its own warranty, servicing and pricing. The offline store's real Google reviews, used to solve the review cold start.
Result. 7.85x ROAS on a May 2024 campaign, around 30 orders a month in the early months, and the store still runs today as The Timekeepers.
Link. [ LINK: the live store ]

The problem

Selling through marketplaces cost margin on every watch. A direct store only keeps that margin if buyers have a reason to skip Amazon.

Two more constraints shaped the store.

Range. More than 25 brands, priced from under ₹5,000 to above ₹60,000, had to share one catalogue without the premium lines losing weight.

Buying habits. Most buyers shop on a phone and expect cash on delivery, and COD orders carry a higher risk of returns and cancellations.

Overview

25+ international watch brands, already selling on every marketplace in India.

Bizotico is a Bengaluru distributor that holds Indian distribution rights for more than 25 international watch brands, including Scuderia Ferrari, Roamer, Obaku and Earnshaw. Its watches sold through Amazon, Myntra, Flipkart and Tata Cliq, and through retail partners.

An earlier D2C store, Zotick, never became a real sales channel. I redesigned it as the Bizotico store, and it went live across 2023 and 2024. It still runs today as The Timekeepers.

Groundwork

What marketplaces cannot offer is what only a distributor has.

This part was catalogue and marketplace analysis, not buyer research, and I would rather name it than dress it up. What I worked from was the full product range, the marketplace listings, and the offline store's reviews, which turned out to be the closest thing to a buyer's voice I had.

Catalogue breakdown. I mapped every brand's products by movement, strap, style and price, which showed where brands overlapped and where the gaps were.

Price-band mapping. I grouped the range into four bands: below ₹20K, ₹20 to 40K, ₹40 to 60K and above ₹60K. Each band became its own collection.

Top brands by style. I split the lead brands by style so each could get its own homepage row, and grouped lesser-known brands for discovery.

Competitive review. Marketplaces win on price and delivery. They cannot offer the distributor's own warranty, its servicing, or direct pricing. That gap became the store's whole position.

Sitemaps and flows. I mapped the key journeys: browse by brand, browse by price, gifting, ad to product page, and product page to checkout.

Key decisions

Three decisions that gave buyers a reason to come direct.

01

Four ways into one catalogue.

What happened

One catalogue had to hold a ₹4,000 fashion watch and a ₹60,000 Swiss automatic without either one dragging the other down.

The decision

Buyers could start from the brand, the watch type, the budget or the person they were buying for. Brand gets an A to Z menu grouped into columns. Type covers Swiss Made, automatic, mechanical and quartz, plus movement and strap filters. Price uses the four bands. Audience covers For Him, For Her, Gift Guide, Best Sellers and New In. On the homepage, top brands get their own rows, and smaller labels sit under "Offbeat brands you wish you knew".

Every new product now has to be tagged across all four facets, which is real recurring work for the client's team in exchange for easier discovery.
02

A reason to buy direct.

What happened

A new store starts with no reviews, and buyers cannot try a watch on through a screen. Both are trust problems, not layout problems.

The decision

The store was positioned around what only the distributor can offer: a two-year warranty backed by Bizotico, a personalised watch service station, and discounts straight from the distributor. A trust bar at the top of every page states returns, COD and warranty. An authorised partner block names the marketplaces Bizotico supplies, as proof of authenticity.

I solved the review cold start by bringing in the offline store's Google reviews: real names, Local Guide badges, and comments about service and delivery that any buyer can verify. On the product page I added real on-wrist videos and to-scale product images next to the studio shots, so buyers see the actual size and finish before purchase. Warranty, authenticity, EMI, care and returns sit in expandable sections.

Naming the marketplaces on our own store reminds buyers that the marketplaces exist. We kept the block because a first-time buyer's first question is whether the watch is real.

03

Ten per cent off prepaid, to take the sting out of COD.

What happened

COD is what buyers expect, and COD is also what generates returns and cancellations.

The decision

COD stays available, because removing it would cost orders outright. Prepaid orders get 10% off, to pull buyers across and cut the returns that come with COD. That discount costs margin on every order that would have been prepaid anyway, and it is still cheaper than the returns. WhatsApp handles pre-purchase questions, because that is where these buyers already ask them.

Campaigns and pricing

One fixed ad frame, so one designer could cover 25+ brands.

Creative template. Every ad used one frame: a maroon corner block, the logo, an offer tag and the price. I led one graphic designer, and the frame is what made the volume possible: he could turn out creatives for every brand quickly, and every ad was recognisably Bizotico. The ads do look alike, which we accepted for speed and recognition.

Ad to product page. Each ad linked straight to the page of the watch it showed, with the same image and the same price. The buyer lands on the exact product, one tap from the cart.

Pricing presentation. Deep discounts, up to 80%, were limited to fashion brands like Lee Cooper. Premium lines like Roamer and Earnshaw stayed between 5% and 20%, so they never looked like clearance stock.

[ IMAGE: ad creatives on one template across brands, and the Diwali sale banners ]

Design system and build

A system the client runs without a developer.

One component library covers the home, brand, collection and product page templates, in deep maroon, black and white, with one accent colour kept for actions and trust badges. Maroon carries the brand across the store, the ads and the testimonials.

I built it in Shopify, with every component mapped to a theme section. Adding a brand means creating a collection and a banner, with no developer needed.

Outcome

7.85x ROAS on a May 2024 campaign, and still live more than two years later.

About ₹53,000 in ad spend against ₹4,16,131 in net sales. Around 30 orders a month in the early months. More than 25 brands online under one system, which the client runs without developer support.

Reflection

What I would do differently.

Set stricter template tiers from day one. Brand-led IA worked, but it is maintenance-heavy. A fixed tier system for premium and fashion brands would have cut per-brand setup time.

Measure the trust layer. The reasons-to-buy-direct model is the core of this work and I never instrumented it. Next time I would track product-page-to-checkout drop-off and the COD to prepaid split, before and after launch.
