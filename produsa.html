Produsa

Turning a website build into a conversation.

Product Designer and Builder · Produsa Labs · Feb to Oct 2026

In short

A shop owner should be able to get a real website by talking, in their own language.

Role. Product Designer and Builder at Produsa Labs, in a team of four, February to October 2026.
The decisions that matter. The chat can only say "done" when the page has actually changed. The agent says plainly what a static site cannot do, and offers the thing that works instead. The whole build runs inside a messaging app, in multiple languages.
Result. 600+ sites built in the first two months after the August 2026 launch.
Link. [ LINK: the live product ]

Where this starts

A bakery owner uploaded her photos and was told "done" three times before they appeared.

That came out of the alpha test, and only because I read the full chat transcripts instead of the session summaries. The summary did not show it. The transcript did, three times, over a page that had not changed.

We named the pattern false confirmation: the system saying something was true when it was not. Almost every failure in the test traced back to it, and it set the agenda for the rest of my work on Produsa.

Overview

An agent interviews the owner the way a developer would scope a project, then builds the site.

Produsa builds a full website through a conversation. The agent reads the brand from references, builds the site, and lets the owner refine it by chatting, in a web studio or over a messaging app. It launched in August 2026, and I worked on it from February until October.

[ IMAGE: the studio, chat on the left, the live site building in the centre ]

The problem

For a small business owner in a tier-2 or tier-3 Indian city, a good website is out of reach.

AgenciesDrag-and-drop builders
Expensive quotes and weeks of back-and-forth Expect the user to know what a hero section or a grid is
Technical vocabulary the owner does not speak English-first interfaces
A site the owner cannot edit afterwards The owner still makes every design decision alone

The people we designed for run a bakery, a bearings distributor, a cafe. Most are not fluent in English, design or development language. If agents do the whole build, the cost of a good website falls far enough for them to afford one.

The alpha test

Four owners, one week before launch, and a very short list of ways the system lied.

Before the August launch we ran a structured usability test with four real business owners: a steel trader, a bearings distributor, a cat cafe and a boutique bakery.

Discovery tested well. Owners could not answer "pick a type scale", but they could answer "who is this for?" and "show me two sites you like", so that is what the interview asks, and the agent turns the answers into design decisions.

Everything else that failed, failed the same way. The chat said "done" to edits it never applied. It invented clients, prices and testimonials, even when the brief said not to. A build that had died kept animating its progress bar toward 100%. One owner asked for a cart and checkout on a static site, and the system added buttons that did nothing, then said the cart would "switch on when you ship it for real".

Key decisions

Four calls that decide whether a person can trust the machine.

01

"Done" only when it is done.

What happened

The chat used to decide it had succeeded based on what the model said, or because a version got saved.

The decision

I moved the success check to the result. The reply can only claim a change if the page itself changed. For images, the system checks that the image data is on the page, not just that an upload tool ran.

Sometimes the chat now says "I could not apply that" where it used to say "done". For one message it reads as a weaker assistant, and it is the change that fixed false confirmation.

Plate 01. The confident lie, beside the useful admission.

02

Say what a static site cannot do.

What happened

A finished Produsa site is static. It cannot run a cart, checkout, payments or logins. The system was promising all four.

The decision

The agent refuses those requests plainly and offers what works instead: an order-by-message button on each product, or a quote button that opens the enquiry form with the item filled in. I also found the "switches on when you ship" promise written into the system prompt itself, and removed it everywhere. Saying "I cannot build a live checkout" can lose a sale in the moment, and a button that does nothing costs more a week later.

03

Progress that cannot lie.

What happened

A build takes minutes, and the wait lied in both directions. One version froze at 20% for 15 minutes while the build ran fine underneath it. Another kept climbing toward 100% on a build that had already died.

The decision

Those are two problems, so they got two fixes. The bar is driven by real build events, so it survives a refresh, a reconnect or a missed event, and it stops at 90 to 95% until the build finishes, which keeps a slow build from looking like a finished one. When the system needs the user, every agent card switches to "Waiting for input".

A build that has stopped is not a progress problem, and no honest bar can cover it, so failure got its own screen: "something went wrong on our side, your work is saved, try again in a minute". Loading, waiting and error were each designed as separate states, the chat is never silent, and every recovery is logged, so a calm screen never hides a real outage from the team.

Plate 02. Narrate the wait, never invent the state.

04

The whole build over chat, in the user's language.

What happened

The owners we designed for already live inside a messaging app, and most of them do not work in English.

The decision

The full pipeline runs inside that messaging app, in multiple languages. A shop owner can scope, build and edit a real website without installing anything or using English.

A chat thread is a much smaller canvas than the studio, and output quality still varies by language. We took both to remove the two biggest barriers: an app to learn and a language to speak.

Behind the chat

Specialist agents, each with one job and a defined handoff.

Research, art direction, content, build, then a QA pass. When quality drops, the team can see which stage failed. On the two calls that change the outcome, art direction and whether a brief is feasible, two agents argue it out and the losing argument is kept.

At my studio, Bleed and Margins, I had led a team through website projects for D2C brands, and every project ran the same way: research the brand, study the competitors, set the creative direction, write the content, build, then review. So the agent structure came naturally. Each step became a specialist agent with its own skill file and handoff. Designing the pipeline mostly meant writing down a process I already ran with people.

The user never sees any of this. Internal states collapse into one plain message: still working.

What we refused

Each of these was considered, and most were partly built before we cut them.

What we cutWhy it was temptingWhy it wentWhat we did instead
[ 1 ]"Built in 30 seconds" in marketing A strong ad hook A real build takes minutes. False speed breaks trust and risks ad-policy rejection Marketing states the real build time
[ 2 ]Rupee prices on the build button Feels transparent Money at the point of action makes owners hesitate on every build Credits on the button, rupee comparison in the wallet
[ 3 ]Silently swapping in a generated image Keeps the build from failing when a photo cannot be used The owner believes it is their photo. False confirmation again The agent says the photo could not be used
[ 4 ]Greeting owners by first name Feels personal A third of stored names are not first names, so many greetings would be wrong A plain "Hey"

Smaller calls

Four that shaped the rest of the work.

Reference-grounded builds. The agent builds from the owner's own material and sites they like, so a bearings distributor and a cat cafe look like different companies. A weak reference gives a weak build, so I built a curated library across niches to keep the floor high.

Editing by chat. After the first build, owners refine by talking. The model gets an exact map of the current page, so a vague instruction still lands on a real section.

Behavioural checks as a gate. Every generated screen, and Produsa's own interface, is checked against written rules: smart defaults, a live prototype before any signup wall, no hard blocks mid-edit, no fake urgency. A failed check gets one mandatory revision before it ships.

One wallet, no fake discounts. Building, editing and hosting all draw on the same credits, so owners deal with one balance and a plain "where your credits go" view. I pulled the strike-through "was" prices off the pricing page, because those prices never existed.

Outcome

600+ sites built in the first two months after launch.

More than 250 of those are shipped and in use by the owners who made them. That gap between built and shipped is the one the product is really judged on, and it is the number I would watch next.

The product has paying users. The count is not mine to publish.

False confirmation was closed at the source, with a check for each failure case from the alpha test, so the same lie cannot ship twice.

How I used AI

The prompt was a design surface.

I wrote the skill files that define what each agent can do, and set up the web-designer agent to study core design principles before it builds. The agent's voice, its refusals and its grounding rules were written alongside the interface, in the same files. I used Codex to build my parts of the system.

Reflection

What I would carry forward.

Honesty is not enough on its own. Owners want stores, and a static site cannot be one. The refusal is a bridge until the product can do commerce.

Models default to saying yes. Honesty had to be built in against that, and it needs tests to stay in place. Next time I would write the structural checks before the first build, not after a user test.

Language quality is uneven. Reaching tier-2 and tier-3 owners in their own language is the whole point, and the output is still better in English. Closing that gap matters more than polishing the English version.
