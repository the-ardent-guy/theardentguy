Produsa: a website for people who can't make one

Produsa builds websites through a chat, on the web or on WhatsApp. You tell it about your business, it asks a few questions, and it designs and builds the site.

My role
Site architecture, UI/UX, training the agents, agentic orchestration, and how the chat behaves. Two developers built the full dev and the pipelines.
Timeline
February to October 2026. Launched August 2026.

The problem

Lots of people need a website and can't make one. They know they need it, and often what it should say. But every route asks for something they don't have. Coding needs skill and time. Agencies need money. Website builders need design sense.

And nobody really wanted "a website". They wanted to be taken seriously and found online.

How we found out

Before building anything, I had run a design agency for eighteen months. The same conversation kept coming up: someone needed a site, roughly knew what they wanted, and had no way to get it that wasn't too expensive, too slow, or meant learning to code.

Then I spoke to about forty people, spread across very different niches: a 3D designer, several homemakers with side businesses, a cafe and stall owner, people who wanted portfolios, and one corporate buyer. I picked different kinds of work on purpose.

What we found

A site meant credibility. Everyone said it differently, but they wanted to look professional and be easy to find.

Time and money stopped small owners. The homemakers had no time to learn web tools and no budget for a freelancer, even for one page. The cafe owner had a paper brochure and just wanted a simple page so people could find him. An agency was way too much for that.

Some knew exactly what they wanted. The corporate buyer paid twice, for Framer and for a designer to build on it. Their problem was getting it built, not deciding what to build.

DIY with AI failed on direction. One person said, "AI will take you on a ride if you go in the web-coding direction." People didn't know what to ask for, so they ended up with a mess.

Creatives refused templates. The 3D designer paid about Rs. 2,500 for a Claude subscription and learned new tools just to avoid a template site. We left fully custom creative work out of the first version, since it pulls against speed. But it set a rule for everyone: the output can't look like a template.

The main lesson: guide people, don't hand them a blank box. A blank prompt is where bad sites come from. The product had to act like a good designer interviewing a client.

What we built

Guided questions. The owner says what they want in their own words. Then the chat asks a few questions about the business, the audience, and the one thing a visitor should do. It only asks what the owner alone knows. The agents make the design calls.

WhatsApp, for owners like the cafe owner. Many small owners already have a brochure or one-pager. They share it on WhatsApp, the bot asks the guided questions, and sends back a prototype link. Then it sends a link to the studio, where they can edit and publish. Very little typing, no code, no agency.

A build that works like an agency. The build follows the order my agency worked in: research, niche, brand design, prototype, website. Each step is an agent that checks its work before passing it on.

Telling the agents what not to do. We first thought a huge library of references would make designs more unique. It didn't. Designs got better when we told the agents what NOT to do: guardrails, constraints, and the character of each niche. A bakery and a corporate firm get held to different standards. That's what keeps sites from looking like templates.

A chat that tells the truth. Owners can't judge a design, so they believe the chat. In testing, the first version said changes were done before they showed up on the page. Now it only confirms a change once it's live. It gives people something to watch while the site builds, says when a step is slow, and treats a failed step as a normal part of the process. Work is always saved.

Fixes from the alpha. We gave the product to 55 to 60 test users from different trades. I watched sessions, and a feedback board sat inside the product. Two problems came up most. The progress bar stuck at 92%, so now it follows the real build and says what it's doing. And owners sent customers the preview link instead of publishing. Now previews lock after ten days, and payment starts ten days after a site goes live.

Tokens, not a fixed plan. Building, editing and hosting all cost tokens. The first site is free with 15 tokens. After that, a build costs 10 and hosting costs 10 a month. The balance shows in the chat, so owners see the cost before they act.

What happened

In the first two months after launch, owners made 500+ builds and 290 sites went live. Owners have started paying to keep their sites hosted.
