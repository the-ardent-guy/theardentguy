Hasta: a palm reading that feels true, not flattering

Hasta reads a photo of your palm and writes you a reading. The reading is free. The full manuscript costs ₹99, once.

My role
I built it 0 to 1 on my own: the research, the knowledge base, curating the responses, and the UI/UX.
Timeline
One month. Launched August 2026. Live at hasta.produsa.app.

Hasta started as a one-month experiment to test one idea from the research: people want a believable, private mirror of themselves more than a flattering prediction.

The problem

Almost nobody I spoke to cared whether palmistry is true. They came for a feeling. Mostly reassurance, sometimes a warning, sometimes a better sense of who they are.

The options they had weren't working. Human palmists made people feel judged. Apps were full of flattery, and flattery reads as fake. Nobody was giving them something they could trust privately and still want to share.

How we found out

Before building anything, I spoke to about twenty people in four groups of around five: IT professionals, financially independent women, homemakers, and Gen Z.

I asked them if they believe in palmistry, when they last had their palm read, and what they ask about. My first version also asked things like "would you use an app that...". That only got polite yeses. So I changed those questions to ask what people actually did: what they turned to the last time they wanted reassurance, which reading stuck with them, and whether they paid for it or showed it to anyone.

What we found

Belief didn't decide use. Even the skeptics still wanted the reassurance.

Each group wanted something a bit different. IT professionals wanted their worries about career and relationships calmed. Independent women used it as a heads-up, not as fate. Homemakers wanted reassurance about family and kids. Gen Z wanted to learn about themselves. Love came up in every group.

A reading that's too good feels fake. One homemaker was told she'd have a good marriage. It shocked her so much that she doubted the whole thing. Believable beat flattering.

Privacy mattered more than accuracy. Some people felt judged by human palmists. A private space with no judgment was the real edge.

People wanted more than one view. Many asked for both East and West. When two traditions see a hand differently, the reading feels more real, not less.

Gen Z share, but only the good stuff. They'd post a reading only if it flattered them.

What we built

A mirror, not a verdict. This line came straight from the research, and it sits on the landing page. Hasta describes tendencies. It doesn't predict. There are no dates, no doom and no gemstones to buy. We're open that this isn't accurate in a scientific sense. It's self-reflection.

One rulebook from three traditions. I put together a canon from Indian, Western and Chinese palmistry: ten sources, including the Garuda Purana, K.C. Sen's Hast Samudrika Shastra and W.G. Benham's 1900 book. I merged them into one rulebook on purpose, so the same sign always means the same thing. But the disagreements stay visible. Where the schools disagree, the reading says which one says what. Nine conflicts are on record: five have a ruling, three have a lean, and one is still open. The reading never pretends the open ones are settled.

Every claim points at your hand. Each line in the reading names the sign it came from and the book behind it. The free reading also says what we couldn't see. This is how a reading earns trust without flattering you.

Private by default. No sign-up. Your photo is used to write the reading and then deleted.

A short, honest summary. The free reading opens with the five things your hand says loudest and your palm in one sentence. That gives Gen Z something about themselves to repeat, while the full reading stays private.

One price, upfront. ₹99, once, shown before you pay. No subscription and no fear-selling.

No marriage or children. Homemakers asked about family and kids, and love came up everywhere. But the homemaker's story showed how a confident reading about marriage can backfire, and these readings have hurt real people. So I chose not to serve them, even though people ask.

Two features after launch. "Both your hands" reads your writing hand and your other hand side by side. All three traditions say to read both. "Saath" reads two people next to each other and names where they might misread each other. It has no score and no percentage.

What happened

850 readings in the first two months. 4.7% of free readings turned into the ₹99 manuscript, with no subscription and no countdown. Hasta was also #2 Product of the Week on LaunchPad India.
