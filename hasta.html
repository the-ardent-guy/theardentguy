Work

Hasta

Designing an AI palm reader that shows what it read.

Lead Designer, UI/UX/AIX · 1 month · Launched August 2026

In short

In a category that runs on fear and stock paragraphs, can an AI reading be made checkable?

Role. Lead designer on a one-month team build: product research, the canon, UI/UX, the reading output and chat behaviour, the hand card, pricing and the ethics rails. Built with a small engineering team.
The decisions that matter. Every claim names the line it was read from and the book that says so. The model has to declare whether it saw each line clearly, faintly or not at all. One price, on screen before you pay, with no subscription.
Result. 850 readings in the first two months, of which about 40 were paid manuscripts.
Link. hasta.produsa.app

The brief

Palmistry is not science, and Hasta does not pretend it is.

It started as a team exercise, built in one month alongside Produsa and launched in August 2026 on a Produsa subdomain. I picked this category because demand for it is still huge, and it went on to make money.

The design question is the interesting part. Every product in this space either guesses and dresses the guess up as certainty, or sells fear and then sells the remedy for it. So: in a category built on fear-selling, can you design an AI reading that a person can check against their own hand?

Overview

Most palm apps guess. Hasta names the line, and the book, behind every reading.

Hasta reads a photo of your palm through three traditions: Samudrika Shastra, Western chiromancy and the Chinese five-element school. Each reading is written from a rulebook we built out of classical texts, so the model cannot use a term we did not give it. You get a free reading, a full manuscript for ₹99, and a hand card to share.

The problem

Palm reading online in India offers two options, and both fail the user.

AI palm scannersAstrology platforms
Detect three or four lines and return a stock paragraph Readers who charge by the minute
Every reading sounds the same Pricing that leans on fear about marriage, money and health
A free "wow", then a paywall with hidden renewals Gemstone and remedy upsells

That left three problems to solve. Generic readings: most tools read from a handful of features and fill the rest with cold-reading lines that fit anyone. Fear-led monetisation: doom predictions, dated forecasts, remedies to buy, renewals nobody asked for. No sources: nobody says which tradition or text a claim comes from.

The first one turned out to be a supply problem before it was a writing problem. Hand shape plus three lines gives a model very little to work with, so it fills the gap with lines that fit anyone. A larger, accurate vocabulary of signs lets it say something specific instead.

Key decisions

Five decisions, each one a refusal of something the category does.

01

Every claim names its source.

What happened

No product in the category says where a claim comes from, which means no reading can be checked.

The decision

Each part of the reading shows the line or mount it was read from, and which book says so, with a collapsible panel for the full reasoning. The form the model has to fill in makes it declare, for every line, whether it saw that line clearly, faintly or not at all. It cannot describe a line it never saw, because there is nowhere in the format to put it.

Showing the working makes a reading feel less magical, and it is the only version a person can hold up against their own palm.

02

Disagreements between schools stay visible.

What happened

The three traditions do not always agree, and the easy move is to quietly pick one.

The decision

Every conflict goes on a register: nine so far, five with a ruling, three with a recorded lean, and one, the serpent mark, still open. When a reading touches a conflict, it says which school says what. Where all three agree, it says that too, as with the health line, which is best when absent in all three. A reading that admits an open question sounds less certain, and it is the version a person can check.

03

Genericness is measured.

What happened

Generic output is the category's defining failure, and nobody in it measures the thing.

The decision

One layer of the prompt, "read this hand, not a hand", bans the cold-reading phrases by name. Then every week a second AI model grades a sample of readings on how faithful they are to the rulebook and how specific they are to the hand, and we track that score release by release. It is the one thing we measure, because it is the one thing the category gets wrong.

04

A hand card people want to share.

What happened

Personality types like INFJ spread because people like owning a label about themselves.

The decision

Hasta gives the palm the same role. Each reading produces a shareable hand card with a custom motif and the person's dominant readings, an identity marker drawn from their own hand. The card also carries the privacy promise, so every share repeats it to someone new. A card does compress a full reading into a few lines, which is how it turns into a horoscope label; keeping only the dominant readings, drawn from the actual hand, is what holds it back from that.

05

One price, shown before you pay.

What happened

The category's reliable revenue levers are subscriptions, expiring credits, timers and paywalled results.

The decision

The free reading reads both hands and opens the heart, head and life lines side by side. The full manuscript, at ₹99, adds the fate line, the mounts, the thumb, and what moves between the two hands. There is nothing to sign up for, one price, paid once, and it is on screen before you pay.

The brief ruled out the rest of the category's standard kit, and we held to it: no subscriptions, expiring credits or timers, no results behind a paywall, no signup before the reading, no compatibility scores out of 36, no dated doom forecasts, no gemstone or remedy upsells, and no health or lifespan predictions, which are enforced as a rule in the prompt and checked in the weekly grading.

The canon

Three traditions, merged into one rulebook, so the same sign always means the same thing.

Six traditions were studied and three made it into the product: Samudrika, from the Garuda Purana and K.C. Sen's Hast Samudrika Shastra; Western, from W.G. Benham's The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading, 1900; and the Chinese five-element school, shuxiang. Earth, Air, Fire and Water hand types were kept out, because they date from the mid-twentieth century and cannot be sold as tradition, and DMIT and finger-ratio claims were kept out as pseudoscience.

Merged, that becomes a fixed vocabulary of lines, mounts and classical signs, and the model reads only from it. That restriction is what makes citing a source possible at all.

Smaller calls

Four that shaped the experience around the reading.

Read both hands. In Samudrika the passive hand, prarabdha, holds what you were born with, and the active hand, agami, what you are building. The Chinese and Western schools say the same. Hasta reads both and shows what moves between them.

Deal with the photo before the wait, not after it. Do's and don'ts appear before the camera opens, with an alignment overlay and an upload fallback, and an on-device check flags photos that are too small, dark or blurry before an expensive call. The check is deliberately lenient, because rejecting a usable photo is worse than passing a borderline one. A diagram or a cupped hand gets a polite decline instead of a made-up reading.

Ask the reader. A follow-up chat grounded in the saved reading, so it cannot contradict it or invent a line that was never there.

Privacy at the point of doubt. The photo is deleted after the reading, with no tracking and no ads. The promise repeats on the upload screen, the report and the hand card, because the moment of doubt is the upload.

Visual system

Designed like a kundli book, the hand-kept astrology record many Indian families own.

Cream parchment for the reading, deep brown for the immersive sections, a sindoor red for actions. A small-caps Roman display face for headings and an italic book serif for the reading itself. Filigree corners, Roman numerals, the canon shown as book spines, and Devanagari and Chinese terms set inline with their meaning. The photography is warm and candlelit, and uses real palms.

Outcome

850 readings in the first two months, and about 40 paid manuscripts.

That is a 4.7% free-to-paid conversion, with no subscriptions, no fear-selling and nothing hidden behind a timer. Built in one month, launched August 2026, live at hasta.produsa.app.

What I did on the AI side

Who wrote the canon, and who wrote the voice.

I gathered the source material: classical texts, transcripts of books and lectures, and voice recordings of practising pandits. The synthesis into one report, The Shastras of the Hand, was AI-assisted research that I commissioned, read and edited. What entered the rulebook, what stayed out, and how conflicts were ruled were my calls.

I wrote the voice, the banned phrases and the grounding rules alongside the interface, and treated the shape of the model's output as part of the UX, because it is the thing that decides whether a reading can cite a source or refuse a bad photo.

Reflection

Distribution is the next design problem.

The product works. The hand card is the first bet on reach, and the next step is measuring how many readings start from a shared card.
