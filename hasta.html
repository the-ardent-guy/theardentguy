Work

Hasta

Designing an AI palm reader that shows what it read.

Lead Designer, UI/UX/AIX · 1 month · Launched August 2026

Overview

Most palm apps guess. Hasta names the line, and the book, behind every reading.

Hasta reads a photo of your palm through three traditions: Samudrika Shastra, Western chiromancy and the Chinese five-element school. Frontier models run each reading from a canon compiled out of classical texts. You get a free reading, a full manuscript for ₹99, and a hand card to share.

We built it in one month and launched in August 2026. It converts 4.7% of free readings into paid manuscripts, without subscriptions or fear-selling.

A note on the category

Palm reading is still a grey area as a product.

Hasta started as a team drill, and I picked this category because demand for it is still huge. It went on to make money. Everything below is an attempt to build inside a category that mostly runs on fear, without using any of the fear.

The problem

Palm reading online in India offers two options, and both fail the user.

AI palm scannersAstrology platforms
Detect three or four lines and return a stock paragraph Readers who charge by the minute
Every reading sounds the same Pricing that leans on fear about marriage, money and health
A free "wow", then a paywall with hidden renewals Gemstone and remedy upsells

That left three problems to solve. Generic readings: most tools read from a handful of features and fill the rest with cold-reading lines that fit anyone. Fear-led monetisation: doom predictions, dated forecasts, remedies to buy, renewals nobody asked for. No sources: nobody says which tradition or text a claim comes from.

Problem statement How might we make an AI palm reading that feels specific to the person, shows where every claim comes from, and earns money without fear?

Research

Readings sound the same because they draw from too few features.

Market teardown. I studied Indian astrology platforms like AstroTalk and Astroyogi, along with the AI palm-scanner apps, for the monetisation patterns to avoid.

Canon study. I directed a study of six palmistry traditions and supplied the source material: deep research, transcripts of classical books and lectures, and voice recordings of practising pandits. It was compiled into a report, The Shastras of the Hand, which I reviewed.

The key finding. Hand shape plus three lines gives the model very little to work with. A larger, accurate vocabulary lets it say more specific things. Specificity was a data problem before it was a writing problem.

What to keep out. Earth, Air, Fire and Water hand types date from the mid-twentieth century and cannot be sold as tradition. DMIT and finger-ratio claims are pseudoscience.

The canon

Ten sources, merged into one rulebook, so the same sign always means the same thing.

Of the six traditions studied, three made it into the product.

Samudrika: the Garuda Purana, chapter LXV on the auspicious marks, and K.C. Sen's Hast Samudrika Shastra. About 116 entries.

Western: W.G. Benham's The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading, 1900. About 185 entries.

Chinese: shuxiang, the five-element school.

The rulebook loads into the model as cached ground truth, with a fixed vocabulary of lines, mounts and classical signs. The model can only read using these terms, which is what makes citing sources possible at all.

Key decisions

Five decisions, each one a refusal of something the category does.

01

Every claim names its source.

What happened

No product in the category says where a claim comes from, which means no reading can be checked.

The decision

Each part of the reading shows the line or mount it was read from, and which book says so, with a collapsible panel for the full reasoning. The output schema makes the model declare, for every line, whether it saw it clearly, faintly or not at all, so it cannot describe a line it never saw. The user can check the reading against their own hand.

The trade-off Showing the working makes readings feel less magical. Being checkable mattered more.

Trade-off plate 01. The schema will not let it describe what it did not see.

02

Disagreements between schools stay visible.

What happened

The three traditions do not always agree, and the easy move is to quietly pick one.

The decision

Every conflict goes on a register: nine so far, five with a ruling, three with a recorded lean, and one, the serpent mark, still open. When a reading touches a conflict, it says which school says what. Where all three agree, it says that too. The health line is best when absent, in all three.

The trade-off A reading that admits an open question sounds less certain. It is also the version a user can check.
03

Genericness is measured.

What happened

Generic output is the category's defining failure, and nobody in it measures the thing.

The decision

One layer of the prompt, "read this hand, not a hand", bans specific cold-reading phrases. A weekly eval harness uses an independent model to score every reading for faithfulness, distinctiveness and genericness, and the scores are tracked release over release.

The trade-off Every release costs extra eval runs. Generic readings are the category's biggest failure, so it is the one thing measured.
04

A hand card people want to share.

What happened

Personality types like INFJ spread because people like owning a label about themselves.

The decision

Hasta gives the palm the same role. Each reading produces a shareable hand card with a custom motif and the person's dominant readings, an identity marker drawn from their own hand. The card also carries the privacy promise, so every share repeats it to someone new.

The trade-off A card compresses a full reading into a few lines, which risks reducing it to a horoscope label. Keeping only the dominant readings, drawn from the person's actual hand, keeps it specific.
[ IMAGE: the hand card, with its custom motif ]
05

One price, shown before you pay.

What happened

The category's reliable revenue levers are subscriptions, expiring credits, timers and paywalled results.

The decision

The free reading reads both hands and opens the heart, head and life lines side by side. The full manuscript, at ₹99, adds the fate line, the mounts, the thumb, and what moves between the two hands. There is nothing to sign up for, one price, paid once, and it is on screen before you pay.

The trade-off This gives up the category's most reliable revenue levers. It still converts at 4.7%.

Other decisions

Seven smaller calls.

Read both hands. In Samudrika the passive hand, prarabdha, holds what you were born with, and the active hand, agami, what you are building. The Chinese and Western schools say the same. Hasta reads both and shows what moves between them.

Guide the photo before it is taken. Do's and don'ts appear before the camera opens, with an alignment overlay and an upload fallback. A bad photo is caught before the wait, not after it.

A lenient on-device check. Photos that are too small, dark or blurry get flagged before an expensive AI call. Some borderline photos still reach the model, because rejecting a usable photo is worse.

Snap, Read, Keep. About three minutes from start to reading. The wait shows the steps of the reading, and the result is kept as a manuscript.

Ask the reader. A follow-up chat grounded in the saved reading, so it cannot contradict it or invent a line.

Refuse bad inputs. A diagram or a cupped hand gets a polite decline instead of a made-up reading, and error states stay in Hasta's voice.

Privacy at the point of doubt. The photo is deleted after the reading, with no tracking and no ads. The promise repeats on the upload screen, the report and the hand card.

Category norms we dropped

Each of these is standard in the category, and each was ruled out in the brief.

[ 2 ]Results behind a paywall High conversion pressure Users pay before knowing if it works A free reading of the three major lines
[ 3 ]Signup before the reading Captures leads A wall before any value Nothing to sign up for
[ 4 ]Compatibility scores Kundli matching runs on scores out of 36 A number turns a relationship into a verdict Saath: two people, two hands, no score
[ 5 ]Doom and dated forecasts Fear sells readings It is the fear-selling we set out to avoid Tendencies only, no dates
[ 6 ]Gemstone and remedy upsells Standard revenue on astrology platforms Selling a fix for a fear you created No gemstones
[ 7 ]Health and lifespan predictions Users expect the life line to mean length of life A wrong reading could cause real harm Enforced as a rule in the prompt and the evals
[ 8 ]Elemental hand types and DMIT Familiar, and they sound scientific A modern invention, and pseudoscience Kept out of the canon

Visual system

Designed like a kundli book, the hand-kept astrology record many Indian families own.

Palette. Cream parchment #F6EEDC for reading, deep brown for the immersive sections, sindoor red #A33B2A for actions, and ochre for labels.

Type. A small-caps Roman display face for headings, and an italic book serif for the reading itself.

Motifs. Filigree corners, Roman numerals, and the canon shown as book spines. Devanagari and Chinese terms sit inline with their meaning.

Photography. Warm, candlelit hands. Real palms and real lines.

Outcome

4.7% free-to-paid conversion, with no fear-selling, subscriptions or dark patterns.

850 readings in the first two months. Built in one month, launched August 2026, live at hasta.produsa.app.

How I used AI

The prompt was a design surface.

The canon was compiled with deep research from the source material I gathered: transcripts of classical books and lectures, and recordings of practising pandits. Frontier models run the readings, with the rulebook loaded as cached ground truth.

I wrote the voice, the banned phrases and the grounding rules alongside the interface, and treated the output schema as part of the UX. Reading quality is checked weekly by an eval harness running an independent model.

Reflection

Distribution is the next design problem.

The product works. The hand card is the first bet on reach, and the next step is measuring how many readings start from a shared card.
