Supaca

Designing trust for a private network of supercar owners

Supaca is a members-only app for verified supercar owners in India, where clubs run their drives and members RSVP with the car they're bringing. The beta is live, with real owners on the waitlist. This case study covers the v1 design, which I led end to end.

Overview

TeamMe on design, with the engineering team building the product
TimelineNovember 2024 to November 2025 (v1 design), beta now live

Supaca only works if every member knows everyone else is a real owner. The hardest part was designing a gate strict enough to make membership mean something, while keeping joining simple.

The problem

Supercar owners in India already had clubs, but no product built for them. As creative director at one of those clubs, I saw how they actually ran: on scattered WhatsApp groups. There was no way to verify who owned a car, drives were organised through forwarded messages, high-value sales ran on trust, and expensive cars sat parked for weeks with nowhere to go.

How might we give verified owners a private place to belong, where the main reason to open the app is the next drive?

Research

Through my club, I had direct access to owners and organisers, and tested ideas with them throughout. I mapped real clubs and their access rules (open, private, referral-only, women-only). Three findings shaped the design:

  1. Owners would only join a network where every member was verified.
  2. Owners wanted somewhere to drive. The event was the reason to come back.
  3. Clubs already existed. The product had to bring them in instead of replacing them.

I also flagged the cold-start risk early, and we agreed on one rule: never fake a member, an attendee or a listing.

Process

I set four principles before any screen: every member is verified by a human, members never pay, nothing is shared without consent, and every path leads to the next drive. I then fixed the information architecture, so every screen had a defined slot and new features could be added without restructuring navigation. I wireframed the core flows and tested them with owners before visual design.

Key decisions

01

Two access tiers in place of five permission profiles

Four archetypes plus pending applicants implied five permission profiles. I designed two tiers instead. Full members are approved with at least one verified car and see the members' area. Limited users see sellers as anonymous, have no DMs, and see no member data. Signup kept three plain entry points: Owner, Club Owner and Vendor.

Why: Separate rules per profile would have scattered access logic across every screen. Following Tesler's law, the complexity moved into one rule no user has to reason about.

Trade-off: Two tiers give some edge cases less nuance, in exchange for every screen behaving predictably.

02

Verification through the garage

A member proves who they are by verifying their car. One registration document proves both identity and ownership, an admin approves it, and until then the app stays locked.

Why: One document covered both checks, so members went through a single step. Because the gate does the work, members never pay.

Trade-off: Verification adds friction at signup, and for an exclusive network that friction is what makes membership worth having.

03

Privacy that fails closed

Members join a club only by choosing to or by an owner's approval. A private club you can't access returns "not found" instead of "access denied", because "denied" confirms it exists. When tiers mix, people stay anonymous until someone chooses to reveal who they are, and member avatars use initials.

Why: Auto-adding members to clubs that matched their car would have been convenient, but on a trust product it would have broken consent.

04

RSVP with your car

Every part of the app leads to the event page. When members RSVP, they choose which car they're bringing, and "Who's coming" shows each attendee with a verified badge and a colour-coded pill for their car's make.

Why: This one screen delivers both findings from research: belonging, through seeing who's coming, and exclusivity, through the verification behind every name.

05

Clubs that exist before their owners arrive

Real clubs are pre-seeded. A club owner claims theirs or creates one, then invites members, so the invite does the vetting and members never land in an empty room.

Trade-off: Club data has to be curated and maintained before anyone asks for it.

06

Keeping money out of the member experience

Members never pay. Club owners pay for their tools, and if a plan lapses, only the owner's dashboard locks while the club stays live. A member-fee toggle exists for owners, off by default.

Why: Charging members to join would have put a price on the one thing that made membership valuable.

Supporting decisions

  • Filters in bottom sheets instead of a Vendors tab, keeping each page to one job through progressive disclosure.
  • Familiar patterns like a five-tab bottom nav and story-style carousels carry new ideas such as verification, following Jakob's law.
  • One meaning per colour: one brand accent, and green means only "verified".
  • Plain language: "You approve every request" replaces a settings matrix.

Outcome

  • v1 designed end to end over a year, from research to every screen
  • The beta is live, with real owners joining through the waitlist
  • One access rule in place of five permission profiles
  • The v1 structure held, and the team rebuilt it into today's beta

Reflection

  • Set the structure before the screens. Fixing the information architecture first gave new features a place to go without redesigning navigation.
  • The edge cases carried the trust. Who can see a private club, whether a member joins anything without asking, and what a stranger sees before verification each mattered more than any single feature.
