Work

Supaca

Designing a private network for India's supercar owners.

Lead UI/UX Designer · Nov 2024 to Nov 2025 · Launched v1

Overview

Verified owners, real clubs, and one screen that shows who is coming to the drive and what they are bringing.

Supaca is a members-only app for verified supercar owners in India. Clubs run their drives and events on it, members RSVP with the car they are bringing, and dealers and detailers reach them through a controlled vendor channel.

I designed and launched v1 over a year, from November 2024 to November 2025. The team has since rebuilt it into the beta live today.

[ IMAGE: the current beta's landing page, rebuilt by the team from v1 ]

The problem

Their social world ran on scattered WhatsApp groups.

India has roughly 3,000 to 6,000 supercar owners, with cars worth ₹2 to 20 crore each. None of the existing platforms were built for them.

No verified identity. No way to know who actually owns a car. No structure for events. Drives were organised through forwarded messages. High-ticket sales on trust. Cars and parts changed hands through forwarded photos. Idle cars. Many owners had a crore of machine parked for weeks, with nowhere to take it.

Problem statement How might we give owners a verified place to belong, where the main reason to open the app is the next drive?

Research

An empty network kills a members' club, so we never faked one.

Direct access. As creative director at Boit Club, one of the clubs on the list, I had direct access to owners and club organisers, and could test ideas with the people who would use the product. That is also a conflict worth naming: I was inside one of the communities I was designing for.

Supply mapping. We mapped the founders' list of clubs into real, verified entries, including Boit Club, Club P11, RaceTech, Marque One and Throttle 97. Each club's own access rules, open, private, referral-only, women-only, mapped to how it would appear in the product.

The cold-start risk. The plan was to launch only into real, seeded clubs with real dated events, under one rule: never fake a member, an attendee or a listing. One fake would break the trust the product depends on.

Key decisions

Six decisions, and the trust each one was protecting.

01

Five permission profiles, collapsed into two access tiers.

What happened

Four archetypes plus pending applicants implied five permission profiles: approved owner, club owner without a car, vendor, admin and pending applicant. A separate rule set for each would have scattered access logic across every screen.

The decision

Two tiers instead. Full: approved, with at least one verified car, sees the members' world. Limited: everyone else, where sellers appear anonymous, there are no DMs, and no member data is visible. Signup kept three plain-English doors, Owner, Club Owner and Vendor, and behind them one rule answers every access question. Tesler's law: the complexity has to live somewhere, so it moved into one rule that no screen and no user ever has to reason about.

The trade-off Two tiers are coarser than five profiles, so some edge cases get less nuance. In exchange, every screen behaves predictably.
02

Verification through the garage.

What happened

Proving a person is who they say they are, and proving they own a supercar, are usually two separate problems.

The decision

A member proves who they are by verifying their car. One document, the registration certificate, proves both identity and ownership, and an admin approves it. Approving a member marks all their cars verified. Until then, the app stays locked. Because the gate does the work, members never pay. The badge is the value.

The trade-off Verification adds friction at signup. For an exclusive network, that friction is what makes the membership worth having.
03

Privacy fails closed.

What happened

Every convenience we considered turned out to leak something: a club's existence, a member's identity, a face.

The decision

No silent joins. Members join a club only by choosing to, or by an owner's approval. Matching members to clubs by their car and adding them automatically would be convenient, and on a trust product it would be a breach. A private club you cannot access looks like it does not exist. The app returns "not found" instead of "access denied", because "denied" confirms the club exists. Identity revealed only on purpose. When tiers mix, people stay anonymous until someone explicitly reveals who they are. Initials, not faces. Member avatars use initials, and photography is reserved for cars, clubs and events.

One deliberate exception: login says "No account found for this email" instead of a vague error. At the front door a clear message converts better, and the invite-only copy explains why. A private club is different, because its existence is itself the sensitive fact.

The trade-off Consent costs convenience. A trust product cannot afford the opposite.

04

The hero moment: RSVP with your car.

What happened

A network needs one reason to open the app, and for this community it was never the feed.

The decision

Everything points at the event page. When a member RSVPs, they choose which car they are bringing. The "Who's coming" list shows each attendee with a verified badge and a colour-coded pill for their car's make. That one screen creates belonging, and the verification behind it creates exclusivity.

The trade-off Putting one screen at the centre means every other feature has to earn its place by leading back to it.

05

Clubs exist before their owners arrive.

What happened

An empty network is the fastest way to lose a members' club, and the first club owner to arrive would have found nothing.

The decision

The real clubs from research were already in the app at launch. A club owner claims their existing club instead of building one from nothing, or creates a new one if theirs is not listed. Once claimed, the owner invites members, and the invite does the vetting: a member arrives already known to a club, then verifies their garage. Members land in a club that already has a name, a crest and other owners in it.

The trade-off Pre-seeding means curating and maintaining club data before anyone asks for it. It is the price of never showing an empty room.
06

Who pays, designed into the flows.

What happened

Money anywhere near the members' experience would have undercut the thing the gate was building.

The decision

Members never pay. The verification gate is what makes membership valuable, so there is no fee at the door. Club owners pay for their tools. The first year is free, then ₹25,000 a year for the owner plan. The paywall only reaches the owner. If an owner's plan lapses, only their dashboard is locked, and the club stays live for its members. A member-fee toggle, off by default. Owners can choose to charge their own members; left off, the member flow is untouched.

The trade-off Charging owners instead of members means growth depends on club owners seeing value first. That is why the first year is free.

Other decisions

Five smaller calls.

Information architecture first. I fixed the route map before designing any screen, so every screen filled a defined slot and new features could be added without restructuring navigation.

Filters in sheets, not tabs. A Vendors tab on the Clubs page was rejected. Filters live in bottom sheets, which keeps each page to one job.

Familiar patterns for unfamiliar ideas. A five-tab bottom nav, an Instagram-style marketplace grid and story-style carousels carry new concepts like verification and escrow. Jakob's law.

One accent, one meaning for green. Supaca Red is the only brand colour. Emerald means one thing: verified.

Plain English. "You approve every request" instead of a settings matrix. Labels a non-car person understands.

Outcome

340 verified owners, against a national market of three to six thousand.

Read that number against its ceiling, not against consumer-app benchmarks. Each owner was personally invited by an existing verified member.

v1 launched in November 2025, after a year of research, design and build. Real clubs only, mapped from research, with zero fake members, attendees or listings. One access rule in place of five permission profiles. The invite-only model held without exception.

The foundation held in the way that matters most: the team has since rebuilt v1 into the beta live today, with club onboarding open and a vendor layer on the way.

Reflection

What I would carry forward.

Decide the structure before the screens. Fixing the information architecture first meant new features had a place to go without redesigning navigation.

Trust is built in the edge cases. The decisions that mattered most were small: who can see a private club, whether a member joins anything without asking, what a stranger sees before verification. Each one could cost more trust than any feature could earn back.
