Supaca

Designing trust for a private network of supercar owners

Supaca is a members-only app for verified supercar owners in India, where clubs run their drives and members RSVP with the car they're bringing. The beta is live, with real owners on the waitlist. This case study covers the design, which I led end to end.

Role
Lead UI/UX Designer
Team
Three of us, with the CEO overseeing: me leading design, one other UI/UX designer, and one full-stack developer
Timeline
November 2024 to November 2025, beta now live
Platform
Mobile-first web app
My work
User research, information architecture, the access model, onboarding and verification, visual design, and every screen

Supaca only works if every member knows everyone else is a real owner. The hardest part was designing a gate strict enough to make membership mean something, while keeping joining simple.

The problem

Supercar owners in India already had clubs, but no product built for them.

As a product designer at one of those clubs, I saw how they actually ran: on scattered WhatsApp groups. There was no way to verify who owned a car, drives were organised through forwarded messages, high-value sales ran on trust, and expensive cars sat parked for weeks with nowhere to go.

That set the bar: a network where every member is a verified owner, and the next drive is there when you open the app.

Research

Through my club, I had direct access to owners and organisers, and tested ideas with them throughout.

I mapped real clubs and their access rules (open, private, referral-only, women-only). Three findings shaped the design:

  1. Owners would only join a network where every member was verified.
  2. Owners wanted somewhere to drive. The event was one of the main reasons to come back.
  3. Clubs already existed. The product had to bring them in instead of replacing them.

I also flagged the cold-start risk early, and we agreed on one rule: never fake a member, an attendee or a listing.

Process

I set four principles before any screen:

  • every member is verified by a human,
  • members never pay,
  • nothing is shared without consent,
  • and the next drive is always easy to find.

I then fixed the information architecture, wireframed the core flows, and tested them with owners before visual design.

Key decisions

01

Five ways in cut to three

We started with five: explorer, enthusiast, owner, club owner and partner.

The social wall was the reason, and spotters and enthusiasts would have filled it quickly. It would also have undone the point, because owners don't want an audience.

We cut it to three entry points, owner, club owner and vendor, with one rule behind the gate: you're verified, or you're waiting. Everyone inside either owns the car or serves someone who does.

Trade-off: We gave up the audience the social wall would have pulled in, which was the fastest way to look busy before the network was real.

02

Verification through the garage

A member proves who they are by verifying their car.

One registration document proves both identity and ownership, an admin approves it, and until then the app stays locked.

Trade-off: Verification adds friction at signup, and for an exclusive network that friction is what makes membership worth having. That is effort justification, and we spent it on purpose.

03

Nobody lands in a club they didn't pick

We had a way to make the app look full on day one: drop every Porsche owner into the Porsche club in their city, and everyone else into one generic supercar club.

We didn't do it. It would have added strangers to each other's clubs with nobody agreeing to it, and an in-group nobody consented to join stops being one. The clubs that already exist here are drawn by area and by who knows whom.

Members join by choosing to, or by an owner's approval. Where verified and unverified people meet, nobody is named until they choose to be, and avatars are initials.

04

RSVP with your car

Much of the app leads to the event page.

When members RSVP, they choose which car they're bringing, and "Who's coming" shows each attendee with a verified badge and a colour-coded pill for their car's make.

Outcome

  • Designed end to end over a year, from research to every screen
  • The beta is live, with real owners joining through the waitlist
  • Five ways in cut to three, with one verification rule behind the gate
  • The beta runs on the structure I designed, and the vendor marketplace is the part being built on it now

Reflection

  • Set the structure before the screens. Fixing the information architecture first gave new features a place to go without redesigning navigation.
  • The edge cases carried the trust. Who can see a private club, whether a member joins anything without asking, and what a stranger sees before verification each mattered more than any single feature.
