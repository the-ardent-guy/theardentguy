Supaca

Designing a private network for India's supercar owners.

Lead UI/UX Designer · Nov 2024 to Nov 2025 · Launched v1

In short

A few thousand supercar owners ran their entire social world through forwarded WhatsApp messages.

Role. Lead UI/UX Designer, November 2024 to November 2025. Research, information architecture, the access model and every screen. The engineering team built it.
The decisions that matter. One access rule in place of five permission profiles. A member proves who they are by verifying their car. Privacy fails closed, so a club you cannot access returns "not found" rather than "access denied".
Result. v1 launched in November 2025 with 340 verified owners, every club real, no fake members or listings.

The one screen

You do not RSVP to the drive. You RSVP with the car you are bringing.

A network needs one reason to open the app, and for this community it was never going to be a feed. Everything in Supaca points at the event page.

When a member RSVPs they choose which car they are bringing. The "Who's coming" list shows each attendee with a verified badge and a colour-coded pill for their car's make. Before anyone wrote a line of that screen, it had already decided the rest of the product: if the car is the thing you bring, the car has to be proven, and proving it has to gate everything else.

Overview

Verified owners, real clubs, and a vendor channel kept at arm's length.

Supaca is a members-only app for verified supercar owners in India. Clubs run their drives and events on it, members RSVP with the car they are bringing, and dealers and detailers reach them through a controlled vendor channel.

I designed and launched v1 over a year, from November 2024 to November 2025.

The problem

Their social world ran on scattered WhatsApp groups.

India has roughly 3,000 to 6,000 supercar owners, with cars worth ₹2 to 20 crore each. None of the existing platforms were built for them.

No verified identity. No way to know who actually owns a car. No structure for events. Drives were organised through forwarded messages. High-ticket sales on trust. Cars and parts changed hands through forwarded photos. Idle cars. Many owners had a crore of machine parked for weeks, with nowhere to take it.

"I don't want to deal with dealers or agents. I want to sell it very discreetly."

A collector, during discovery

That sentence is why the marketplace shows sellers anonymously to anyone outside the gate, and why no part of the product reveals who a person is without them choosing to.

Research

An empty network kills a members' club, so we never faked one.

Direct access, and a conflict. As creative director at Boit Club, one of the clubs on the founders' list, I could put ideas in front of owners and club organisers directly. I was also inside one of the communities I was designing for, which is worth naming rather than selling as neutral research.

Supply mapping. We mapped the founders' list of clubs into real, verified entries, including Boit Club, Club P11, RaceTech, Marque One and Throttle 97. Each club's own access rules, open, private, referral-only, women-only, mapped to how it would appear in the product.

The cold-start risk. The plan was to launch only into real, seeded clubs with real dated events, under one rule: never fake a member, an attendee or a listing. One fake would break the trust the product depends on.

Key decisions

Five decisions that hold the event page up.

01

Five permission profiles, collapsed into two access tiers.

What happened

Four archetypes plus pending applicants implied five permission profiles: approved owner, club owner without a car, vendor, admin and pending applicant. A separate rule set for each would have scattered access logic across every screen.

The decision

Two tiers instead. Full: approved, with at least one verified car, sees the members' world. Limited: everyone else, where sellers appear anonymous, there are no DMs, and no member data is visible. Signup kept three plain-English doors, Owner, Club Owner and Vendor. Instead of five rule sets, every screen asks one question: does this person have a verified car?

Two tiers are coarser than five profiles, so the edge cases get less nuance than a product manager would want. Every screen behaves the same way in exchange.
02

Verification through the garage.

What happened

Proving a person is who they say they are, and proving they own a supercar, are usually two separate problems.

The decision

A member proves who they are by verifying their car. One document, the registration certificate, proves both identity and ownership, and an admin approves it. Approving a member marks all their cars verified. Until then, the app stays locked. Verification is what makes the membership worth having, so we never charged members for it.

03

Privacy fails closed.

What happened

Every convenience we considered turned out to leak something: a club's existence, a member's identity, a face.

The decision

No silent joins. Members join a club only by choosing to, or by an owner's approval. Matching members to clubs by their car and adding them automatically would be convenient, and on a trust product it would be a breach. A private club you cannot access looks like it does not exist. The app returns "not found" instead of "access denied", because "denied" confirms the club exists. Identity revealed only on purpose. When tiers mix, people stay anonymous until someone explicitly reveals who they are. Initials, not faces. Member avatars use initials, and photography is reserved for cars, clubs and events.

One deliberate exception: login says "No account found for this email" instead of a vague error. At the front door a clear message converts better, and the invite-only copy explains why. A private club is different, because its existence is itself the sensitive fact.

04

Clubs exist before their owners arrive.

What happened

An empty network is the fastest way to lose a members' club, and the first club owner to arrive would have found nothing.

The decision

The real clubs from research were already in the app at launch. A club owner claims their existing club instead of building one from nothing, or creates a new one if theirs is not listed. Once claimed, the owner invites members, and the invite does the vetting: a member arrives already known to a club, then verifies their garage. Members land in a club that already has a name, a crest and other owners in it. The cost sits on our side: someone has to curate and maintain that club data before a single person asks for it.

05

Who pays, designed into the flows.

What happened

Money anywhere near the members' experience would have undercut the thing the gate was building.

The decision

Members never pay. There is no fee at the door. Club owners pay for their tools. The first year is free, then ₹25,000 a year for the owner plan. The paywall only reaches the owner. If an owner's plan lapses, only their dashboard is locked, and the club stays live for its members. A member-fee toggle, off by default. Owners can choose to charge their own members; left off, the member flow is untouched.

Charging owners instead of members means growth depends entirely on club owners seeing value first. That is why the first year is free.

Smaller calls

Three that kept the system legible.

Information architecture first. I fixed the route map before designing any screen, so every screen filled a defined slot and new features could be added without restructuring navigation.

Filters in sheets, not tabs. A Vendors tab on the Clubs page was rejected. Filters live in bottom sheets, which keeps each page to one job.

One accent, one meaning for green. Supaca Red is the only brand colour. Emerald means one thing, verified, and appears nowhere else. Labels stay in plain English: "You approve every request" instead of a settings matrix.

Outcome

340 verified owners, against a national market of three to six thousand.

Read that number against its ceiling, not against consumer-app benchmarks. Each owner was personally invited by an existing verified member.

v1 launched in November 2025, after a year of research, design and build. Real clubs only, mapped from research, with zero fake members, attendees or listings. One access rule in place of five permission profiles, and the invite-only model held without exception.

The team has since rebuilt it into the beta that is live today, with club onboarding open and a vendor layer on the way.

Whose screens these are

Every screen in this case study is mine, from v1.

The team has continued the product since, and the later additions, the vendor layer among them, are theirs rather than mine.

Reflection

What I would carry forward.

Decide the structure before the screens. Fixing the information architecture first meant new features had a place to go without redesigning navigation.

Trust is built in the edge cases. The decisions that mattered most were small: who can see a private club, whether a member joins anything without asking, what a stranger sees before verification. Each one could cost more trust than any feature could earn back.
